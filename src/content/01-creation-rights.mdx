The moment you record a song, you create two separate assets. Most money problems in music come from people not knowing this.

<TwoHundredPercent />

## The two copyrights

**The master** is the recording itself: the actual audio file. Whoever paid for the session usually owns it. For a self-funded artist, that's you.

**The composition** (also called *publishing*) is the song underneath: the melody and the lyrics. It belongs to whoever wrote it, even if someone else records it later.

Both can be owned by different people, sold separately, and paid by different companies. That's why a song is "200%."

## Who counts as a writer?

Writers are people who contributed to the melody, lyrics or chords. A producer who built a beat or a topline, yes. A mixing engineer who balanced levels, normally no. A session player who performed a part you wrote, normally no. The line is fuzzy, so settle it **the same day**, out loud, with everyone in the room.

## Write it down before you leave the studio

Agreements made from memory turn into arguments the first time a song gets traction. A split sheet is a one-page record of who owns what:

- every collaborator's legal or stage name,
- their share of the master (adds up to 100%),
- their share of the publishing (adds up to 100%),
- signatures and the date.

## Samples and interpolations

Using someone else's recording needs permission for **both** sides: the master owner and the song owner. Re-playing a melody yourself still needs the songwriter's permission. "It's only a few seconds" is not a legal rule. Clear it before release, because a claim after release usually means lost income.

## Protect your work

- Keep dated copies of project files, lyrics and bounces. They show who made what and when.
- Registering with the national copyright office adds real legal protection if there's ever a dispute. Look up the current fees and forms at the <ResourceLink id="copyright_office" />.
- Get collaborators' agreements in writing even when you trust them.

<Callout title="Not legal advice">
This is a plain-English starting point. For a contract with real money or ownership on the line, talk to an entertainment lawyer.
</Callout>
